# Micro Journal Rev.3: Nadia - a Keyboard for Your Phone or Tablet

A small mechanical keyboard that connects to your phone, tablet or PC with a single cable. It turns your phone into a distraction-free writing setup, and the cable tucks away into a small pocket when you are done.

### What you get

* **Made for the sofa:** compact enough to rest on your lap or across your thigh, stable enough to stay with you through the whole thought.
* **A phone holder that is part of the design:** when the middle panel flips, it holds your phone, so you see the keyboard and the screen together.
* **A real keyboard:** 48 mechanical keys with hot-swappable switches that you can change for your own favorites.
* **Plug and type:** it shows up as a standard USB keyboard. No battery, no pairing, no app.
* **Two rotary knobs** that move the cursor by default, and can be set to anything you like.
* **Make it yours:** change any key and the knobs with the free Vial software.

#### What is in the DIY Kit

* 3D printed parts
* Raspberry Pi Pico (RP2040)
* Keyboard PCB, 48 keys
* 2x O-ring 18x13.2x2.4
* 2x EC11 rotary encoders, 15mm half handle, for the knobs
* 2x IN4148 diodes
* Short USB-A to USB-C cable, and a USB-C to USB-A OTG cable
* Various M3 and M2 hex screws, and heat inserts

#### Please note

* **Only the Fully assembled version includes switches and keycaps.** The DIY Kit and the version without switches come with an empty keyboard, whatever the options on the listing show.
* For the version without switches, use any Cherry MX compatible switches (3-pin or 5-pin).
* **Connecting to an iPhone or iPad:** some older models require a "Lightning camera adapter".

## What it is, and what it isn't

Micro Journal Rev.3 is a **keyboard**. It has no screen. Whatever device you connect it to, such as a phone, tablet or PC, is where your writing appears and is saved.

**Good for**
* Typing longer messages, notes and drafts on your phone or tablet.
* Writing on the sofa, in bed, or on the go.
* Using as a compact keyboard with a PC.

**Good to know**
* Connect it with a **USB-A to USB-C data cable**. A USB-C to USB-C cable does not work in most cases.
* To connect to a USB-C phone, you also need the OTG adapter (included in the DIY Kit).
* To charge your phone while you type, use a USB hub with power input.

## The story behind it

Sometimes I sit on the sofa. The television is on, though it belongs to the kids. I sit nearby. A thought drifts in, and I pull out my phone.

I wanted to make a keyboard that looks good while keeping the phone holder naturally attached to it. Functional, yet feeling like a coherent part of the design. When the middle panel flips, it holds the phone. A natural transition that guides the user to see the keyboard together with the phone.

Sometimes I wonder if anyone really wants to write on a phone. Yet from time to time I find myself in that exact situation. Sitting on a sofa, the phone in my hand, needing to write something longer than a few words. It usually begins with a simple reply to an email. Or a long comment on social media that refuses to stay short. The screen keyboard appears. My thumbs begin their small dance across the glass. Slowly the message forms. It always works.

Still, in those moments I often feel the same small wish returning. I want a keyboard that can rest on my lap or across my thigh. Something stable enough to stay with me through the whole thought. Something that lets the words arrive without fighting the screen. I wish I had this keyboard.

### Specs at a glance

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Microcontroller** | Raspberry Pi Pico (RP2040) |
| **Keyboard** | 48 keys in a 4x12 grid, hot-swappable, Cherry MX compatible (3-pin or 5-pin) |

| **Firmware** | QMK with Vial, customizable without coding |
| **Connection** | USB keyboard. Use a USB-A to USB-C data cable. No USB Power Delivery. |

### Sourcing the parts

Almost everything in this build is a standard, widely available part that you can buy online. The keyboard PCB is the exception. It is the only component that cannot be bought on the market, because it is a custom board and PCB manufacturers normally require a bulk order.

To make a single build possible, I work with my PCB supplier, Elecrow, who keeps the board in stock. You can order a single piece here:

* [Micro Journal 48 Keys Keyboard PCB (Elecrow)](https://www.elecrow.com/micro-journal-diy-kit-48-keys-keyboard-pcb.html)

You can also have the board made yourself. The manufacturing files are in the [48 keys keyboard PCB design files](https://github.com/unkyulee/micro-journal/tree/main/shared/keyboard-48).
