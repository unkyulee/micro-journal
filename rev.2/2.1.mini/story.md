# Micro Journal Rev.2.1.mini: Why Rev.2 disappeared, and why it came back.

Since Rev.2 was retired in 2025, I've been asked countless times whether it would ever come back. Rev.2 was popular, much more popular than I had anticipated. There was a time when I would open a batch for orders and everything would sell out in just 10 seconds. It was exciting and, I must admit, quite satisfying.

Then there was one person.

For nearly six months, he contacted me almost every day. Some messages were about genuine technical issues. But many were about things I would consider rather trivial, like asking how to tighten the hinges, and then asking me to tighten them for him, which required shipping the device back. I tried my best to accommodate him. At some point I had rebuilt and sent him two replacement units, but the messages continued. Weekdays, weekends, evenings, even during my vacation. It felt as though I was expected to be available at any moment, for six months straight. I don't use the word lightly, but I think I can describe the experience as harassment.

Eventually, I offered him a full refund, even after having sent the replacement units. He refused, and the messages continued. When I muted the conversation, he contacted Tindie support, where the same daily routine carried on until he finally received his refund. But that wasn't quite the end. He posted a rather horrible, accusatory message about me in the Reddit community, and deleted it the following day. Then he contacted me again to explain that he had taken the post down because he was a generous person, and because he understood how artists like me worked. I didn't quite know what to make of that. I still don't.

What surprised me most was what happened to me afterward. I noticed that whenever a conversation with a customer reached its third or fourth reply, I began to feel anxious, even when the person was perfectly kind and the question completely reasonable. Some part of me was expecting another endless conversation, another situation I couldn't escape. So I began avoiding messages. Not because I didn't care about the people writing them, quite the opposite, but because I had become afraid of opening a conversation I might not be able to close. It was no longer healthy for me, and eventually I decided to retire Rev.2.

There was something I learned from this experience, and it was about popularity. When your work reaches more people, you naturally encounter more kinds of people. Most are wonderful. Some are difficult. And occasionally, you meet someone whose expectations are simply impossible for you to meet.

I never particularly tried to make Micro Journal popular. I started building these devices because I enjoyed making them, and I enjoyed sharing the process, the mistakes, the improvements, and eventually seeing other people build and use them. Of course, when Micro Journal started getting attention, I was happy. I welcomed it, and I would be lying if I said otherwise. I liked seeing people excited about something I had created. What I hadn't quite understood was everything that came along with that popularity.

These days, I don't try to reach more people. In fact, I find myself doing the opposite. I announce things quietly, almost as if I were hiding a release rather than promoting it. I don't want Micro Journal to reach everyone. I want it to reach people who understand what it is. An independent, open-source, DIY writing device, made by someone who enjoys designing, experimenting, and sharing what he learns along the way. It isn't a polished commercial product backed by a company with a customer service department. It's a project made by one person, and I would like to remain that person, someone who enjoys creating things, not someone who spends his days afraid of the next message in his inbox.

So Rev.2 disappeared in 2025, and now, in 2026, it is coming back. Why? Because I want closure. For a long time, Rev.2 had become associated with an unpleasant period of my life, which was rather unfair to the device itself. After all, I loved this build. I loved its little keyboard, its Linux environment, and the peculiar feeling of having a tiny computer made for writing and almost nothing else. So many wonderful people enjoyed it, too, and it seemed a shame to let one difficult experience become the final chapter of something that had brought me so much pleasure.

So I started designing it again. It's not quite the same device. It has improved stability, a bigger battery, a revised enclosure, and the lessons learned from all the builds that came after it. But it carries the same spirit. I'm not bringing Rev.2 back to repeat its popularity. I'm bringing it back because I want to enjoy making it again.
